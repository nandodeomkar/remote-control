<objective>
Prove the whole dismiss vocabulary — including the two cases the ownership model was
designed to make free — and record the predictive-back baseline the phase must not move.

Purpose: `canPop` is the single most dangerous expression in this phase. Written as
"anything is raised" it inverts D-13-02 and traps the user on a screen they cannot leave
without dismissing first; written as a constant it stops back exiting the app from the
hub and kills Android's back-to-home animation. Both failures are silent. This plan
turns each into a red test.
Output: `test/shell/summon_back_guard_test.dart` and
`test/shell/predictive_back_baseline_test.dart`.

    **Scenario 1 — ownership (D-13-02).** Pump the shell with the hub as the root route wrapped in
    a guard. Raise a surface on the hub. Push a second guarded route standing in for a tool screen.
    Assert the surface is still raised over the pushed route. Fire a system back: the pushed route

    nothing raised the hub is poppable and the app exits. Assert each of the three steps

    on "anything raised" would have dismissed on the first back and left the user unable to leave
    the tool screen without dismissing first.

    **Scenario 2 — the orphan (D-13-03).** Push a guarded tool route, raise a surface **from that
    route**, then pop the route programmatically the way its app-bar back arrow does — not with a
    back gesture. Assert the surface is still raised and now un-owned: a system back from the hub
    exits the app with the surface still up, and tapping the raised surface's own bar icon still
    lowers it. Attach the reason: this is the deliberate trade recorded in D-13-03 — ownership
    transfer was offered and declined, so the absence of a matching live guard id is the whole
    mechanism and no transfer machinery exists to test.

    **Scenario 3 — no retained route.** Assert that after the owning route is popped and disposed,
    the controller's ownership predicate returns false for **every** live guard id, and that the
